Otter Tutor

How four of us turned a webcam, a tiny screen and a laser into a calculus tutor in one night, and the decisions that got us there.

How we built it

We're Otter Tutor. Instead of walking through features, we'll walk through the six decisions that shaped the build. The website has the technical details.

Decision 1 · the rule

Point at the mistake. Never give the answer.

Chatbots already solve calculus for you. Before we picked a single part, we agreed the tutor would only show you where to look.

✓Which line is wrong
✓What you were doing there
✗The method
✗The answer

Rule one: no answers

This rule came first and it drove everything else. If the tutor can't explain, it needs another way to show you the mistake. That's where the laser came from.

Keep the robot simple

We changed boards three times on paper. The heavy work, vision and voice, runs on the laptop. The robot only needs to show a face and aim, so a cheap ESP32 with Wi-Fi was enough.

Decision 3 · give it a face

The screen we already had became the face.

We dropped the LED-matrix idea and drew an otter on the 2.8″ screen. Six moods show what it's doing: idle, listening, thinking, talking, happy, confused.

A face, not a box

A face makes a correction feel friendly instead of judgmental. Our first boot came out with inverted colors, the otter was blue. We fixed it and matched the final golden otter.

Decision 4 · show, don't describe

A laser points at the line.

1f(x) = x² · sin x
2f′(x) = (2x sin x − x² cos x) / sin² x

“Line 2, second term” is hard to follow out loud. So the robot clips onto the webcam: camera and laser see the same board, and two servos move the dot.

Point, don't explain

Because of rule one, the robot can't explain the fix. A dot on the board is faster than any sentence. Clipping onto the webcam keeps the laser and the camera aimed at the same thing.

Decision 5 · make it printable

Designed in CAD. Printed overnight.

  • Measured twiceScreen window cut to 51.2 × 70.4 mm
  • One arm, not twoWe cut the second, decorative arm
  • 7 partsSized for a friend's Bambu P2S

7 parts, one printer

We had one night and a borrowed printer, so every part had to print without fuss. We measured the screen twice because the first window was too small, and we skipped a second arm we didn't need.

Build in parallel

We agreed early on one small message between laptop and robot: which face, where to look, laser on or off. With that fixed, the brain and the body could be built at the same time without waiting on each other.
